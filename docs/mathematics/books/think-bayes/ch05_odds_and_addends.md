## 1. Odds

- **Odds in favor** — the ratio of the probability an event occurs to the probability it does not; a 75% chance of winning is odds of three to one (`3:1`) because winning is three times as likely as losing.
- **Odds against** — for low probabilities it is more common to quote the odds against; a 10% chance of winning is `9:1` against.
- **Converting probability to odds** — `Odds(p) = p / (1-p)`.
- **Converting odds to probability** — from decimal odds, `Probability(o) = o / (o+1)`; from a numerator/denominator pair, `Probability2(yes, no) = yes / (yes + no)`.
- **Intuition at the track** — picture the crowd: if 20% think your horse wins and 80% don't, the odds in favor are `20:80` = `1:4`; if odds are `5:1` against, five of every six expect a loss, so the win probability is 1/6.

## 2. The Odds Form of Bayes's Theorem

- **Ratio of posteriors** — for two hypotheses A and B, dividing the probability form of Bayes's theorem for each gives `p(A|D)/p(B|D) = [p(A) p(D|A)] / [p(B) p(D|B)]`; the normalizing constant p(D) cancels out.
- **Odds form** — when A and B are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, the prior and posterior ratios become odds, yielding `o(A|D) = o(A) · p(D|A)/p(D|B)`: **the posterior odds equal the prior odds times the likelihood ratio.**
- **Why it matters** — this form is the most convenient way to do a Bayesian update on paper or in your head, since it needs only the prior odds and the likelihood ratio.
- **Cookie problem revisited** — bowls chosen at random give prior odds `1:1`; the likelihood ratio for vanilla is `(3/4)/(1/2) = 3/2`; so posterior odds are `3:2`, i.e., probability 3/5 — the same answer as the Chapter 1 table method.

## 3. Evidence and the Bayes Factor

- **Data favor a hypothesis** — intuitively, data favor a hypothesis if the hypothesis is more likely in light of the data than before; the cookie's vanilla flavor moves Bowl 1 from 50% to 60%, so it is evidence for Bowl 1.
- **Bayes factor** — the likelihood ratio `p(D|A)/p(D|B)`, equal to the ratio of posterior to prior odds; it makes "evidence" precise:

| Bayes factor | Meaning |
|--------------|---------|
| > 1 | data more likely under A → odds for A increase |
| = 1 | data equally likely under either → odds unchanged |
| < 1 | data less likely under A → odds for A decrease |

- **Oliver's blood problem** — from MacKay: two people left blood at a crime scene, of types 'O' (frequency 60%) and 'AB' (frequency 1%); suspect Oliver has type 'O'. Do these traces give evidence that Oliver was one of the people who left blood?
- **Likelihood if Oliver is guilty** — Oliver accounts for the 'O' sample, so the data's probability is just the chance a random person supplies the 'AB' sample: 1%.
- **Likelihood if Oliver is innocent** — two random people must supply the 'O' and 'AB' samples; either order works, so the probability is `2 × 0.6 × 0.01 = 1.2%`.
- **Counterintuitive conclusion** — the Bayes factor is `1/1.2 < 1`, so the blood data are actually evidence *against* Oliver's guilt: data consistent with a hypothesis are not necessarily in favor of it. If Oliver explains the common 'O' event, the rare 'AB' event is left unexplained; if he doesn't, there are two chances to find an 'AB' person, and that factor of two makes the difference.

## 4. Distributions as Units of Computation

- **Update is fundamental but not alone** — `Update` (prior + data → posterior) is the core Bayesian operation, but real problems also need scaling, arithmetic, max and min, and mixtures; this chapter covers addition and max.
- **A distribution is a data structure** — computationally, a distribution is any structure mapping a set of possible outcomes to their probabilities; Downey urges treating a distribution as a fundamental unit of computation, not just a container.
- **Pmf vs. Cdf** — both represent the same information and are interconvertible; they differ in performance, with some operations faster on a Pmf and others faster on a Cdf.
- **Non-Bayesian aside** — apart from the odds form of Bayes's theorem, this chapter is not specifically Bayesian, but understanding distributions and operations on them underpins all Bayesian analysis.

## 5. Addends

- **Motivating example** — in Dungeons & Dragons, a character attribute is generated by rolling three 6-sided dice and summing them, so you might want the distribution of that sum.
- **Two ways to compute a sum's distribution** — *simulation* draws random samples, adds them, and accumulates the distribution of simulated sums; *enumeration* takes all possible pairs of values and computes the distribution of sums exactly.
- **SampleSum (simulation)** — takes a list of distributions and a sample size n, generates n random sums via `RandomSum` (which calls `Random()` on each distribution and adds), and returns a Pmf; it is only approximately correct, growing more accurate but slower as n increases.
- **Pmf.\_\_add\_\_ (enumeration)** — overloads `+` to enumerate every pair of values from two Pmfs, incrementing the sum's probability by the product of the pair's probabilities; so `d6 + d6 + d6` computes the exact distribution. Its run time is proportional to `len(self) * len(other)`.
- **Independence assumption** — `Pmf.__add__` assumes the selections from each distribution are independent (good for dice); dependent cases would require extending it to conditional probabilities.

## 6. Maxima

- **Motivating example** — when generating a D&D character you care about the *best* attribute, so you want the distribution of the maximum.
- **Three ways to compute a maximum's distribution** — *simulation* samples and takes the max; *enumeration* pairs all values and takes the max of each pair; *exponentiation* converts to a Cdf for an efficient closed-form computation.

| Method | Run time |
|--------|----------|
| Simulation (`SampleMax`/`RandomMax`) | proportional to n samples; approximate |
| Enumeration (`PmfMax`) | proportional to m² per pair (km² for k selections) |
| Exponentiation (`Cdf.Max`) | proportional to m (m log m for `Pmf.Max`) |

- **Simulation and enumeration mirror addends** — `RandomMax`/`SampleMax` are the sum code with "sum" replaced by "max," and `PmfMax` is `Pmf.__add__` with `v1+v2` replaced by `max(v1, v2)`.
- **Cdf shortcut** — since `CDF(x) = p(X ≤ x)`, the max of independent draws is ≤ z only if every draw is ≤ z, so `CDF3(z) = CDF1(z) · CDF2(z)`; for k draws from the same distribution, `CDFk(z) = CDF1(z)^k`. `Cdf.Max(k)` simply raises each cumulative probability to the kth power.
- **Best-attribute example** — `three_exact.Max(6)` gives the distribution of the best of six attributes (each the sum of three dice); the chance of generating a character with at least one attribute of 18 is about 3%.

## 7. Mixtures

- **Mixture** — the distribution that results when the outcome comes from one of several distributions chosen at random; e.g., picking a random die from a box and rolling it yields a mixture of uniform distributions with different bounds, which fits no simple model but is straightforward to compute as a Pmf.
- **Box inventory example** — the box holds 5 four-sided, 4 six-sided, 3 eight-sided, 2 twelve-sided, and 1 twenty-sided die; the dice are not equally likely, so each must be weighted by its chance of being selected.
- **Unweighted mixture** — for two equally likely dice (d6, d8), loop over the dice and over each die's outcomes, using `Pmf.Incr` to add up the contributions, then normalize.
- **Weighted mixture** — build a `pmf_dice` mapping each die to its selection weight (the inventory counts), then for each `(die, weight)` and each `(outcome, prob)` increment the mixture by `weight * prob`. Values 1–4 are most likely (every die produces them); values above 12 are rare (only the 20-sided die reaches them).
- **MakeMixture** — `thinkbayes` encapsulates the weighted algorithm as `MakeMixture(pmf_dice)`; it is reused in Chapters 7 and 8.
