## 1. What This Book Is About

Martin Russ frames the third edition with a deliberate distinction: this is a book about *sound synthesis and sampling*, not about synthesizers and samplers. The "what, who, where and when" of particular instruments is left to manufacturers' brochures and the internet; what the book pursues is the **how and why** — how sounds can be made, and how they fit into the larger process of making music. The coverage is therefore concerned with underlying principles and stays abstracted away from specific manufacturers wherever possible, with named instruments confined mostly to the "Examples" sections at the end of each chapter.

**Sound synthesis** is defined broadly as the process of producing sound, whether by generating it electronically or mechanically or by reusing and processing existing sounds. Under this definition, all musical instruments are "synthesizers" — the human vocal tract, a violin, and a clarinet all produce sound by essentially synthetic methods — even though popular usage has narrowed the word to mean an electronic instrument capable of a wide range of (often "synthetic") sounds. Russ insists throughout that the technology is only a set of tools: the creative skill of the performer is what keeps music from becoming mundane.

Several frameworks recur across the whole book and are worth holding in mind. The first is that every synthesizer divides into a **control interface** (which sets the parameters that define the sound) and a **synthesis engine** (which interprets those parameters and produces the output), with a layer of **abstraction** — a model or metaphor — between them so that a player need not understand the inner workings. The second is the **source-and-modifier model** (also called the "excitation and filter" model, borrowed from speech research), in which a raw sound source is shaped by a modifier. The third is the **"Produce, Mix, Record, Reproduce" sound cycle**, a loop that every piece of recorded music passes through, often several times, and which technology has progressively folded into single devices and made invisible. The book's arc traces sound-making from the physical, through analogue and hybrid electronics, to digital and finally software synthesis — a steady "removal of the physical" and "integration onto the computer."

## 2. The Language of Sound

Before the techniques, Russ lays down the shared vocabulary of acoustics, electronics, and digital audio that the rest of the book assumes.

### 2.1. Acoustics and Hearing

Sound is vibration: pressure waves of compression and rarefaction travelling through a medium. The rate of pressure change is the **frequency**, measured in hertz, and the time for one cycle is the **period**. Frequency and musical **pitch** are closely related but not identical — frequency is objective and measurable, while pitch can be subjective. A4 is conventionally 440 Hz, an octave is a doubling of frequency, and the equal-tempered semitone is the twelfth root of two (about `1.0595`); a semitone divides into 100 **cents**, of which the ear can detect roughly five.

The **fundamental** is the lowest major frequency of a sound — the pitch one would whistle. **Harmonics** are integer multiples of the fundamental (the harmonic series), while **overtones** or **partials** that are not integer-related give bell-like or noisy character. **Timbre** — "tone color" — is set by which harmonics are present, at what levels, and how that content evolves over time. Two signals can reinforce or cancel depending on **phase**; two slightly different frequencies produce **beating**, a slow wobble in level whose rate equals their difference, which analogue synthesists prize for "livening up" a sound. Loudness is described logarithmically in **decibels** (about 1 dB is just audible, about 10 dB sounds twice as loud), and the change of a sound's volume over time is its **envelope**, conventionally broken into attack, decay, sustain, and release.

### 2.2. Digital Audio and Sampling

Digital technology represents sound as numbers rather than continuous voltages. **Sampling** is the conversion from analogue to digital: the signal is measured at regular intervals set by a sample clock, each measurement is turned into a number, and the numbers are stored (a CD takes 44,100 samples per second). The reverse — number to voltage to audio — is **sample replay**, which Russ stresses is the basis of nearly every digital synthesizer, however the numbers were produced. The encoding of each value as a number is **PCM** (pulse code modulation), and uncompressed digital audio uses Linear PCM.

Two theoretical limits govern quality. The **Nyquist criterion** requires sampling at least twice the highest frequency present; anything above half the sample rate folds down and masquerades as a lower frequency, a one-way loss called **aliasing**, which anti-aliasing and reconstruction filters exist to prevent. The number of bits sets the resolution: more bits give a wider dynamic range, very roughly 6 dB per bit, so 16-bit audio reaches about 96 dB ("CD quality"). Reducing volume by simply dropping bits introduces grainy **quantisation noise**, which is why volume is better scaled at full resolution. These same artifacts — quantisation noise and aliasing — are, Russ notes, the digital world's own characteristic "dirt," just as tuning drift and noise are the analogue world's.

## 3. The Source-and-Modifier Model

The single most important conceptual tool in the book is the **source-and-modifier model**: a raw sound source produces a basic tone, which a modifier then shapes into the final sound. Russ illustrates it with real instruments, which break into a source of sound, a modifier that processes the source's output, and the controllers a performer uses. A clarinet is the clearest case — the reed (source) produces a harsh tone, and the tube acts like a series of resonant filters (modifier). The model is weaker for a guitar, where the string (source) and body (modifier) are so tightly coupled they cannot be examined in isolation.

This is also where Russ connects synthesis to the physical instruments that preceded it. The **Hornbostel–Sachs** classification, used by ethnomusicologists, organizes instruments by what actually vibrates: **idiophones** (the body itself, as in a marimba or triangle), **membranophones** (a stretched membrane, as in drums), **chordophones** (strings, as in guitar or piano), **aerophones** (a column of air, as in a flute), and the more recent **electrophones** (a loudspeaker, covering electronic instruments). The dimensionality of the vibrating element matters: long, thin one-dimensional vibrators (strings, air columns) resonate strongly and produce a clear pitch, while two- and three-dimensional vibrators give weaker, more complex resonances and more diffuse pitch. Synthesizers sit awkwardly in this scheme — nominally electrophones, but often mathematically modeling one of the other four families.

The source-and-modifier model came first in analogue subtractive synthesis, and because analogue came first, its terminology and metaphors were inherited by every later digital method. Understanding it is therefore the key that unlocks the rest of the book.

## 4. Analogue Synthesis

Analogue electronics represents values continuously, using voltages and currents that directly stand for both the audio signal and the control signals that shape it. The defining innovation of the analogue synthesizer was **voltage control**: instead of mechanical knobs, parameters are set by control voltages (CVs). Because the audio signals are themselves voltages, the same kind of signal serves as both audio and control — one oscillator running at a few hertz is a vibrato modulator, but the same oscillator at a few hundred hertz becomes a sound source.

The dominant commercial method was **subtractive synthesis**: start with a harmonically rich source and filter away unwanted harmonics, then shape the volume envelope. Its building blocks became the standard vocabulary. The **VCO** (voltage-controlled oscillator) is the source, offering waveforms chosen for being easy to describe and generate — sine (fundamental only, useless for subtractive work), triangle, square (odd harmonics, hollow), sawtooth (bright, all harmonics), and pulse, whose harmonic content shifts as its width changes under **pulse width modulation**. The **VCF** (voltage-controlled filter) is the principal modifier, an amplifier whose attenuation varies with frequency; low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, and notch responses change the proportions of harmonics, and **resonance** (a feedback-induced peak at the cutoff) gives the genre-defining sound of a filter swept by a decaying envelope. Push resonance far enough and the filter self-oscillates into a pure sine wave. The **VCA** (voltage-controlled amplifier) is the final stage, its gain driven by an **envelope generator** — most commonly the four-segment **ADSR**, which produces many shapes from just attack, decay, sustain, and release controls. An **LFO** (low-frequency oscillator) supplies cyclic modulation for vibrato and tremolo, and a **sample and hold** circuit, fed with noise, produces the stepped random voltages of a thousand 1970s records.

Subtractive synthesis has a near-opposite, **additive synthesis**, which builds a sound by summing sine waves, grounded in Fourier's 1807 theorem that any periodic waveform can be reproduced from a series of sines. Its weakness is the sheer number of parameters — a 32-harmonic instrument with eight parameters each exceeds 250 controls — which is why practical additive synthesizers tended to be digital. Other analogue methods round out the palette: **amplitude modulation** and **ring modulation** (which outputs only the sum and difference frequencies, producing bell-like and "alien" timbres), analogue **frequency modulation** (powerful but defeated by VCO tuning instability), and **formant synthesis** that emulates the fixed resonant peaks of real instruments and voices.

Analogue instruments came in two forms. **Performance synthesizers** wired the VCO–VCF–VCA path into a fixed topology for fast results and memory recall; **modular synthesizers** left the connections open for great flexibility but were slow to patch, nearly impossible to reconstruct a patch on later, and very limited in polyphony. **Monophonic** instruments played one note (with last-note or low-note priority rules), while **polyphonic** ones stacked several monophonic "voices," whose slight tuning and timbre differences gave the characteristic open "ensemble" sound. The great practical problem was tuning stability: early Moog oscillators drifted with temperature, and a succession of fixes — a special compensation resistor, matched transistor pairs, and finally custom chips — chased the problem until, ironically, that very instability is now deliberately emulated in temperature-stable digital instruments. Landmark instruments map the era: the patchable Moog modular (1965), the Minimoog (1969) that hard-wired the "basic" voice into a portable performance instrument, the expressive polyphonic Yamaha CS-80 (1978), and the Sequential Prophet 5 (1979) that added digital sound storage to analogue voices.

## 5. The Hybrid Era and Samples-and-Synthesis

**Hybrid synthesis** describes instruments that are neither fully analogue nor fully digital. It mattered most during the early-1980s changeover, when digital sound generation and digital parameter control were paired with analogue filtering and enveloping — the right technology applied to each task. The crucial differentiator from pure analogue was a richer raw **source**: digital storage offered waveshapes far beyond the traditional sine/triangle/sawtooth/pulse set.

A continuum of digital sources emerged, differing only in length and repetition. **Wavecycle** oscillators replay stored waveforms, from a single repeated cycle through fixed multi-cycle sequences. **Wavetable synthesis** goes further by selecting the actual waveform on a cycle-by-cycle basis, sweeping or randomly jumping through a "table" of cycles in memory — making it, Russ observes, arguably the general case of which sampling is a special case. The **DCO** (digitally controlled oscillator) was developed to replace the drift-prone VCO with something pitch-stable, while the **DCF** (digitally controlled filter) set an analogue filter's parameters from stored numbers; "digital" became a marketing virtue after the Yamaha DX7, so DCO and DCF displaced VCO and VCF on spec sheets even as the modifier section often remained a simple resonant low-pass filter, keeping the filter-sweep cliché alive.

The lasting outcome of the hybrid recipe was **S&S** (samples and synthesis): a sample-playback source feeding a subtractive VCF/VCA modifier section, with samples stored in ROM as PCM. S&S began as a hybrid (sample-replay DCO plus analogue filters) and ended fully digital, and it became the dominant commercial approach. Russ identifies its characteristic limitation — the **fingerprint problem**: because S&S samples are normally replayed singly rather than sequenced, the source is a fixed set of preset sounds, and each carries a recognizable audio "fingerprint" (a sampled violin still sounds like that violin despite filtering). Escaping it requires more than simple replay — wave-sequencing, cross-fading, or wave-mixing. Even tape-based instruments like the **Mellotron**, with a separate length of tape per key, can be understood as S&S using magnetic tape instead of memory chips. The era's defining instruments include the Fairlight CMI (1979), the wavetable-based PPG Wave (1982), and the Roland D-50 (1987), arguably the first commercial S&S synthesizer (Roland called the technique "LA," linear arithmetic), before the all-digital Korg M1 ended the hybrid period.

## 6. Digital Synthesis Methods

Digital synthesis covers any method using predominantly digital techniques, with the only analogue part often being the audio leaving the DAC (and in software synthesizers, nothing analogue at all). Its great advantage is mathematical precision — repeatability and consistency — but that precision brings its own problems: exact calculations can cancel harmonics that analogue tuning variations would have preserved, so imperfections sometimes have to be artificially reintroduced. The deeper control digital offers always demands a larger investment of effort to use.

The most commercially important digital method was **FM** (frequency modulation), in which the output of one oscillator modulates the frequency of another. Impractical on drifting analogue VCOs, it became usable only with digital technology, following John Chowning's landmark 1973 paper. The oscillator producing the heard tone is the **carrier**, the one modulating it is the **modulator**, and as the modulator's level rises the **modulation index** increases, throwing off more **sidebands** whose amplitudes follow Bessel functions. Integer carrier-to-modulator ratios give harmonic spectra like those of the square, sawtooth, and pulse waves; non-integer ratios give the clangorous, bell-like timbres FM is famous for. Yamaha packaged the technique into **operators** (an oscillator, envelope generator, and digital amplifier) connected in fixed arrangements called **algorithms**, and the Yamaha DX7 (1983) — the first commercially huge all-digital synthesizer, and the host of the first public MIDI test — defined a sound with well under twenty parameters, making FM powerful under real-time control though notoriously hard to program by hand.

Several other methods fill out the digital landscape. **Waveshaping** passes a signal through a non-linear amplifier whose transfer function adds harmonics; Casio's CZ series presented a variant called **phase distortion** as a familiar oscillator-plus-filter. **Physical modeling** builds a DSP mathematical model of how a real instrument works and produces the whole sound in one operation, yielding realistic note-to-note transitions at the cost of deep physics knowledge and large per-patch data; the **Karplus–Strong** plucked-string algorithm and the **digital waveguide** (a delay line with feedback) are its workhorses. **Analogue modeling** (virtual analogue) instead converts analogue circuitry into software — far simpler, and the dominant growth area of the early 2000s, led by the Clavia Nord Lead (1995) — often deliberately degrading "perfect" filters with noise and roll-off so they sound analogue. **Granular synthesis** builds sounds bottom-up from very short enveloped fragments called **grains**, giving shimmering textures. A family of voice-oriented techniques models **formants** — the fixed resonant peaks of the vocal tract — including the **vocoder**, which imposes one sound's spectral envelope on another, and Xavier Rodet's **FOF** (Fonctions d'Onde Formantique), each oscillator generating one formant from a stream of enveloped bursts. Finally, **analysis–synthesis** (resynthesis) samples a real sound, extracts descriptive parameters using tools like the FFT, and recreates it with a synthesis engine — a powerful idea that has repeatedly foundered on the difficulty of offering a usable interface.

## 7. Sampling and Its Editing

A **sampler** is equipment that records a sound, stores it, and replays it on demand — and it is the ability to *record* that distinguishes a true sampler from a synthesizer that merely stores and replays. It is built on three devices used in three modes: the ADC (record), memory (store), and DAC (replay). The key technical division between a sampler and an S&S instrument is the type of memory: S&S uses fixed **ROM**, so its samples cannot be edited and it leans on the synthesizer modifier section, while a sampler uses volatile **RAM**, so its samples can be edited. Over time the two converged, as S&S gained user sample RAM and samplers became largely replay-only devices.

Recorded samples need editing to be usable. **Normalization** sets levels consistently across pitches; **truncation** ("topping and tailing") trims the unwanted material before and after the sound so replay starts cleanly; and **looping** repeats a portion to extend the sustain and save memory. Looping is where most of the craft lies: the splice between a loop's end and start clicks unless level, slope, and rate-of-change are matched, the splice is made at a **zero-crossing**, or the join is **cross-faded** — and even a perfect splice shifts pitch if the cycle time is not maintained. **Time-stretching** and **pitch-shifting** independently change timing or pitch (unlike transposition, which changes both), at some cost in quality. Because large transposition introduces artifacts — extreme cases produce comic "munchkinization," and percussion sounds wrong transposed at all — instruments use **multi-sampling**, spreading several samples across the note range, with a **keymap** recording how samples map to their home and playback pitches.

## 8. Synthesis Moves onto the Computer

The third edition's most heavily revised theme is the migration of sound-making onto general-purpose computers. Russ advances what he calls the **integration hypothesis**: "computers are a one-way street to integration." Being general-purpose, the computer keeps absorbing functions, and **Moore's law** — the doubling of transistors per chip every couple of years — kept making it cheaper and more capable, unlike a toothbrush or a car. The result is that the computer steadily ate the studio: MIDI made instruments easy to connect, the on-screen sequencer beat the multi-track recorder (any tempo without a pitch change), large screens simplified sample editing, and plug-ins removed outboard gear, leaving the **Digital Audio Workstation (DAW)** — sequencer, sampler, synthesizers, effects, mixer, and libraries in one software package.

The enabling invention was the **plug-in**: a standardized software add-on that lets a third party supply functionality the host program's authors never anticipated. The idea matured in Apple's HyperCard and took its name from SuperPaint, but in audio it was Steinberg's **VST** (Virtual Studio Technology, 1996) that mattered: VST 1 added audio effects, VST 2 added MIDI and so enabled **VST Instruments** (plug-in synthesizers and samplers), and VST 3 (2008) was a full rewrite. Rival formats followed — Apple's Audio Units, Digidesign's RTAS, MOTU's MAS — usually tied to a particular host and operating system. Russ argues that, because connecting software instruments became as easy as connecting hardware once was, "plug-ins are the synthesizers of the twenty-first century," and the host software is the enabling **environment**: a virtual, well-equipped studio whose entire configuration can be saved and recalled instantly, something hardware does poorly.

Working on a computer brings its own constraints. **Latency** — the delay between an action and its result — is dominated by the audio output buffer, forcing a compromise between a large buffer (high latency, low CPU load) and a small one (low latency, high CPU load). **CPU load** caps how many virtual instruments and effects can run at once, managed by strategies such as **track freezing** (rendering a demanding part to a sample) and distributing work across cores. Meanwhile MIDI, the protocol that made all of this connectable, has receded: it reached a stable, adequate point and now travels mostly hidden inside USB, while to most users a MIDI file played through a built-in soft synthesizer behaves just like an audio file, its abstraction no longer apparent.

## 9. Techniques for Combining and Shaping Sounds

Beyond producing a single timbre, the synthesist combines and shapes sounds — and here the one player acts simultaneously as **arranger** (choosing timbres), **conductor** (timing and dynamics in context), and **player** (low-level control of how a timbre is produced). Russ fixes the loose industry vocabulary with precise definitions. **Stacking** is a single composite sound from two or more timbres heard together (whether genuine **composites** of different sounds or **doubles** of one sound at several pitches, often detuned for richness). **Layering** is a composite that changes over time as separate layers come and go — classically one sound for the attack and another for the sustain. **Hocketing** sends successive notes of one musical part to different instruments, so that moving a few notes between tracks gives the impression of an elaborate arrangement.

Two independent numbers describe an instrument's capacity. **Multi-timbrality** is how many different timbres it can produce at once, turning one instrument into several virtual ones; **polyphony** is the total number of notes it can sound at once. As a rule, polyphony must equal or exceed multi-timbrality, and using multi-timbrality well demands surprisingly high polyphony, because overlapping and long-release notes consume voices. A **voice** is the part of a synthesizer that plays one note, named after the analogue **voice cards** that capped early polyphony near eight notes. When that limit is reached, **note stealing** must free resources, and the strategies of **dynamic voice allocation** — reserving notes for a melody, prioritizing parts, stealing only from quiet or releasing voices — turn the voice assigner from a simple counter into an intelligent record-keeper, trading complexity against responsiveness.

Two standards and one craft round out the chapter. **General MIDI (GM)**, from 1991, is a shared specification built on the idea that most music can be reproduced from a small standard set of 128 timbres plus a fixed drum map, so that a MIDI file made on one GM device plays much the same on another — "paint by numbers" music in which the file carries both the events and the sounds. **On-board effects** moved from outboard rarities in the 1970s to an integral part of the instrument, evolving from a single global effects bus to true multi-timbral effects where each part keeps its own processing, making the effect part of the modifier section. And Russ offers a consistent editing philosophy: never use a preset sound unedited, never use the same note twice (vary it slightly to emulate human imperfection), and above all hold a clear **mental model** of how the technique makes sound — without one, parameter changes are merely random, as the mostly-unusable output of "randomizing" editors demonstrates.

## 10. Controllers and the Keyboard's Accidental Dominance

Separating the control interface from the sound generator yields two devices: the **expander module**, a keyboard-less synthesizer that is the bare sound producer, and the **controller** that drives it. Because a conventional instrument's controls are fixed by its physics while a synthesizer has no such limits, the choice of controller is free — and it determines how a player's expression reaches the listener. Russ argues the organ-type keyboard became dominant "for all the wrong reasons": it was simply the easiest thing to wire up as control voltages for the first synthesizers, yet it is naturally polyphonic (bad for early monophonic instruments) and offers expression only through attack velocity and after-touch. No single controller does everything, so they are sometimes combined.

**MIDI** is the standard digital control language, but it carries the keyboard's assumptions in its bones: pitch and **velocity** are bound together in note messages, while volume, **pitch bend**, and **modulation** each need separate continuous controllers, and every pitch change is expected to start a new envelope — which makes it poor at the smooth note-to-note transitions of a bowed or fretted string. A controller message is three bytes (identifier-and-channel, controller number, value); the familiar **modulation wheel** is controller 1. **MIDI Learn** spares the user from looking up controller numbers by letting a parameter "listen" for the next incoming controller and bind to it. Beyond MIDI, a string of proposed successors (ZIPI, OSC, mLAN, RTP-MIDI) has appeared, even as MIDI itself increasingly travels invisibly inside USB.

The alternative controllers each have a niche. **Wheels, levers, joysticks, and pressure pads** handle hand control, a pitch-bend wheel sprung back to center and a modulation wheel left where it is set. **Ribbon controllers** sense where a finger presses a strip, suiting pitch bend and glide. **Wind controllers** — from the Lyricon (1974) onward — translate breath into volume and Boehm-style fingerings into pitch, excelling at expressive monophonic melody. **Guitar controllers** are the hardest of all, because extracting each string's pitch is difficult; the most useful payoff of the research has been the **hexaphonic pickup**, which senses each string separately and lets the guitar become a composite of acoustic, processed, and synthesized sound. **3D controllers** sense the hands moving in space, the Theremin being the classic and notoriously difficult example. Compared across the board, the keyboard excels at complex polyphonic performances but is weak on expression; wind controllers give superb monophonic lines but little polyphony; the guitar offers expressive but limited polyphony. Tellingly, few alternative controllers ever become new instruments — most serve to perform pre-recorded music, with the player acting as a kind of conductor.

## 11. The Future of Sound-Making

Russ closes by arguing that synthesis is nearly "complete" in one sense: sound *quality* has reached close to the physical limits of the human ear, so the remaining work lies in refining techniques and, above all, the **parameters used to control** them. **Control is the real bottleneck.** Even when the basic vibration of an instrument has a well-understood mathematical model, real instruments add layer upon layer — supporting structures with their own resonances, many playing techniques, muting and damping, and the performer's varied interactions — each of which complicates both the model and the controller needed to drive it. He notes that other synthesis fields hit the same wall: 3D rendering approaches photorealism but struggles with controlling animation, and speech synthesis sounds natural from analyzed values yet synthetic from plain text. The Theremin stands as both promise and warning — an instrument with just two controls that is nonetheless extremely hard to play — and his own analogy is that he could rent a high-performance sports car but lacks the skill to impress anyone with it: the limit is control, not the engine.

The likely future, he suggests, is not bigger sample sets or marketing acronyms but **flexible, software-based synthesis** combining multiple methods, with the synthesizer increasingly a component inside a larger music-making — ultimately multimedia — system in which the performer is an active, controlling part. **Multi-core processing** will raise power without ever-rising clock speeds, and synthesis and recording will continue to converge on a fully digital path inside the computer. Russ is careful to add that the future is rarely a simple extrapolation: drawing waveforms on acetate once seemed to promise total freedom until the Fairlight revealed its limits, and physical modeling seemed poised to replace sample replay until analogue modeling and a retro fashion won the short term.

Underpinning all of this is a **commercial imperative**: synthesizer evolution has always followed what was cheap, available, and well understood. Analogue subtractive synthesis was chosen in the 1950s as a cost-effective way to make many sounds; FM gave the first cost-effective *digital* sound generation; S&S then maximized cheap memory; and as memory grew cheaper still, sample replay offered such large sound ranges so cheaply that the economies synthesis once provided were no longer needed. The honest question Russ leaves open is whether a modern flexible-architecture stage keyboard, which most users only lightly edit, is "almost not a synthesizer at all" but a sophisticated sample-replay device — and his answer is that the genuine future of synthesis lies in **software synthesis engines inside software studios**, where complexity is limited only by the user's budget rather than the designer's, allowing the esoteric techniques that a mass-market keyboard never could.

## 12. Key Takeaways

- **The subject is the how and why of making sounds, not a catalogue of gear.** Russ keeps to underlying principles, so the ideas outlast any particular instrument.
- **Every synthesizer is a control interface plus a synthesis engine, joined by an abstraction.** The model a player is given matters as much as the technique underneath it.
- **The source-and-modifier model is the master key.** A raw source shaped by a modifier explains real instruments, analogue subtractive synthesis, and, through inherited terminology, almost every digital method that followed.
- **The fundamentals are non-negotiable.** Frequency, harmonics, timbre, and envelope on the acoustic side; PCM, the Nyquist criterion, aliasing, and bit-resolution on the digital side — each technique is a different way of manipulating these.
- **Sound-making evolved physical → analogue → hybrid → digital → software**, a steady "removal of the physical" and integration onto a single general-purpose computer.
- **Each major method has a signature and a weakness:** subtractive's resonant filter sweep, additive's control explosion, FM's clangorous spectra and hard programming, S&S's sample "fingerprint," physical modeling's realism at the cost of complexity.
- **Sampling is defined by the ability to record**, and most of its craft lives in editing — normalization, truncation, and especially click-free looping and multi-sampling.
- **The computer absorbed the studio**, with the plug-in as the pivotal invention; "plug-ins are the synthesizers of the twenty-first century," and MIDI has receded into an invisible layer inside USB.
- **Combining sounds has a precise vocabulary** — stacking, layering, hocketing — and using multi-timbrality well demands far more polyphony than it first appears, making voice allocation a real engineering problem.
- **Control, not sound quality, is the enduring frontier.** Sound has reached the ear's limits; the unfinished work is the interface between performer and instrument, and the future belongs to flexible software synthesis whose ceiling is the user's budget rather than the designer's.
