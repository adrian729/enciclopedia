## 1. Arranging

- **Three layers of control** — synthesis lets one person act simultaneously as **arranger** (choice and control of timbres), **conductor** (precise timing and dynamics of sounds in context), and **player** (low-level control over how a timbre is produced); a human arranger normally has to transfer instructions through a score or the spoken word, but the synthesist changes layer just by changing role.
- **Two ways to build composite sounds** — either wire several separate synthesizers together (a large MIDI-network structure needing a patch bay, hard to store or move) or use one **multi-timbral** synthesizer (implemented in its operating-system software, so a whole stack or layer stores in one instrument's performance memory and travels easily). The chapter calls either source a "synthesizer sound source" or **part** and largely ignores the distinction.
- **Stacking vs. layering (the book's definitions)** — these terms are loosely and interchangeably used in manufacturer literature, so the author fixes them: **stacking** is a single composite sound from two or more timbres heard together; **layering** is a composite sound that changes or evolves with time as separate layers come and go. The three technique divisions are arranging (stacking, layering, hocketing), timbres (multi-timbrality/polyphony, GM, effects), and control (controllers, editing).

  | | Stacking | Layering |
  |---|---|---|
  | Timbres | same (sometimes different) | different |
  | Intervals | unison, octave, fifth | unison |
  | Tuning | often detuned | no detuning |
  | Envelopes | same or similar shapes | different shapes (time separation) |

## 2. Stacking

- **Two types of stack** — **composites** combine more than one sound or timbre at once; **doubles** take one sound and use multiple pitches derived from it. Composites work best when simple but distinctive — using more than two parts can waste polyphony and over-complicate the result.
- **Composite methods** — **additive** (combine simple sounds, like additive synthesis, to escape a technique's limits — e.g. summing two low-pass subtractive sounds for a richer spectrum); **hybrid** (contrasting or complementary techniques: analogue with digital, imitative with synthetic, familiar with alien, additive with FM to counter additive's inharmonic weakness, sample with imitative for "hyper-real" results); **splitting** (split a target into components, recreate each separately, recombine — related to analysis–synthesis and **residual synthesis**, where a similar spectrum is removed from a source and the residual reused).
- **Doubling** — a hi-tech term for reusing the same musical information with a transposition or tuning change, both parts playing together: **detuning** (richer chorused sound, detuning both parts oppositely or just one), **octaving** (thickens but separates into two sounds as the interval grows), **intervals** (parallel pairs, often fifths), and **chording** (parallel chords — a strong effect that can upset the music's harmonization).
- **Alternatives** — stacking two very similar sounds from different sources, like audio **double-tracking**, so slight imperfections of each combine into a richer sound (e.g. two GM string sounds thickened so the result is not obviously from a standard GM source). Guitar **hex pickups** add capability: conventional guitar pickup, synth sound, and hex-pickup sound can be independently processed and placed in a surround field.

## 3. Layering

- **Time separation** — layering normally separates sounds in time, conventionally one sound for the initial attack and another for the sustain, but more broadly any two time-independent sounds. It is especially useful for personalizing preset-heavy equipment, where the more unusual (otherwise "unusable") sounds work better than conventional ones.
- **Layering possibilities** — **percussive and pad** (fast-attack/decay with slow pad, the pad level controlled by staccato/legato playing or the sustain pedal); **opposites** (opposite envelopes giving a sound that dynamically morphs between two timbres); **echo/reverb and dry** (different acoustic "spaces" change a sound's apparent distance and can make poly-rhythmic textures); **pan position** (contrasting pans give dynamically moving stereo); **slow decay and slow rise** (a non-static, evolving "sustain" as the balance between two layers shifts); and **loops** played against pads or other loops for continuous variation.
- **Splits** — assigning different sounds to different keyboard ranges (e.g. piano on the upper half, string bass on the lower for jazz). Less typically, the parts of a split can control different *layers* of a single sound on a note-by-note basis — control more akin to an orchestra than to a keyboard synthesist.

## 4. Hocketing

- **Hocketing** — sending successive notes of the same musical part to different instruments instead of one. With contrasting timbres, pans, effects, or detune, it can make complex arpeggios and accompaniments and give the impression of a detailed arrangement when really just a few notes were moved between tracks and a few tracks copied and pasted.
- **The sequencer process** — listen with one sound source, choose a criterion, select and filter the notes (by order, number, velocity, beat, time…), copy them to another track, assign that track a different sound source, and set the two to contrasting or complementary timbres. Modern sequencers reduce the steps while restricting flexibility — often a useful compromise — and hocketing across more than two instruments is increasingly popular.
- **Hocketing criteria** — **by note sequence order** (also called MIDI note cycling/randomizing; subtle with similar timbres, like splitting parts with contrasting ones); **by note number** (fixed split-point, odd/even for pseudo-inversions, chord splitting around the middle note); **by velocity** (like velocity splitting but edited in the score, good for sounds moving between accompaniment and melody); **by beat or time** (timbre or pan reflects bar position or absolute time — better than "random pan"); **by a controller** (cross-faders for two instruments, joysticks for more; **Legato mode** uses a controller to switch between two looped note patterns, each carrying on where the last stopped).
- **By a note event (key switching)** — hocketing controlled by note events, typically low/high notes or a split played by the left hand while the right hand plays the controlled instrument; gives detailed, velocity-switching-like control without precise playing or volume changes. A related variant, **"Trigger on playing speed,"** uses the repetition rate of playing as a controller, so an instrument sounds only on fast runs — especially effective on a guitar controller. The flexible Karma technology in some Korg synthesizers combines hocketing, arpeggiation, and Legato-mode generation.

## 5. Multi-Timbrality and Polyphony

- **Multi-timbrality** — the ability of a single synthesizer to produce several different timbres at once, turning one physical instrument into several "virtual" ones (with effects, overall control, and sound management usually staying common). It is an extension of stacking: if a key can sound two timbres at once, those timbres can also be played independently.
- **Polyphony** — the total number of different pitches or sounds an instrument can make at any one time. The two numbers are independent, though specs often quote the same figure for both; as a rule **polyphony should equal or exceed multi-timbrality** (a 2-note, 16-part instrument is impossible — it can only ever make two parts).
- **Voice** — an imaginary part of a synthesizer capable of playing only one note, named after the analogue **voice cards** (simplified VCO/VCF/VCA circuits, like independent singers in a choir). Tuning and control problems capped early analogue polyphony near 8 notes, whose minor tuning/timbre variations gave a distinctive open "ensemble" feel — so analogue polysynths were used more for polyphony than multi-timbrality.
- **History of the numbers** — the digital Yamaha DX7 (1983) started mono-timbral but offered 16-note polyphony versus the usual analogue 4-, 5-, 6-, or 8-note; its precise tuning and identical timbres felt very different until the early 1990s, when analogue imperfections began being emulated. Larger polyphony made multi-timbrality effective: 8-part then 16-part multi-timbrality became the standard, and by the 2000s 64- or 128-note polyphony was common in S&S synthesizers, so extra modules were needed for sounds rather than polyphony.
- **Maximum polyphony** — where polyphony depends on synthesis resources, simple sounds give the most notes and complex sounds fewer; e.g. 16 oscillators give 16-note "maximum" polyphony only if each note uses one oscillator, dropping to 8 notes at two oscillators per note and 4 at four. Best sounds tend to use full capability, so the "typical" figure sits between the maximum and minimum.
- **Notes per part and overlaps** — using multi-timbrality needs surprisingly high polyphony (64-note polyphony plays only four pitches across 16 parts). Legato, tied, or overlapping notes each use 2 notes of polyphony, and because music clusters events around beats, peak polyphony can reach double the apparent amount. Long **release** segments make the problem worse: a written staccato can actually play legato because the sound keeps using resources after note-off.

## 6. Note Allocation and Voice Stealing

- **Note allocation (voice assignment)** — how required notes are assigned to available voices. The base method is **cyclic assignment**: notes go to voices in order, wrapping around (on 8 voices, the 9th note steals voice 1). Pure cyclic assignment ignores voice status — it would even steal a sustained note while staccato voices sit idle — so it wastes resources.
- **Note stealing** — when the polyphony limit is reached, "old" playing notes must stop to free resources for "new" ones; the behavior depends on the allocation method.
- **Dynamic voice allocation (DVA)** — strategies that make cyclic assignment responsive to incoming notes and voice availability:
  - **Note reserving** — guarantee a fixed polyphony to a specific part (e.g. 2 notes for a melody so overlaps don't cause stealing); inefficient when that part is idle. An asymmetric split (e.g. 6 and 2 on two detuned timbres at the same pitch channel) can make 8-note polyphony sound like 12 detuned notes.
  - **Part priority** — give melody/drum parts priority so stealing takes from lower-priority parts; more efficient than fixed reservation, but recent notes also need higher time-priority.
  - **Voice / envelope / volume status** — steal only from voices not playing, or in the release/sustain segment, or at lower volume — all less audible than stealing an attack or a loud note.
  - **Repeated note detection** — reassign a repeated pitch to its own voice (retriggering its envelope) so the other voices are undisturbed.
  - **Sustain pedal detection** — held notes stay in the sustain segment; if notes must be stolen, an "oldest-note" cyclic basis is appropriate.
- **The trade-off** — tracking reservation, priority, and status turns the assigner from a simple counter into something keeping detailed per-voice records; this "intelligent" assigner adds software overhead and can slow response, so allocation complexity is balanced against responsiveness.

## 7. General MIDI (GM)

- **General MIDI (GM)** — a shared specification (1991, MIDI Manufacturer's Association and Japan MIDI Standards Committee) extending MIDI on the idea that most music can be reproduced from a small set of common instrumental timbres — in effect a minimalistic "electronic orchestra," marked with a distinctive GM logo.
- **The sound set** — 128 sounds plus a drum-kit mapping, divided into 16 groupings of 8 (keyboards, chromatic percussion, organ, guitar, bass, strings, ensemble, brass, reeds, pipes, synth lead, synth pad, synth effects, ethnic, percussion, sound effects). Percussion is fixed on channel 10; other channel assignments are left to the user. A GM device must be 16-part multi-timbral with a minimum of 24 dynamically allocated notes (16 instrumental, 8 percussion), though modules don't always meet this.
- **Purpose: predictable playback, not synthesis** — every maker's GM set is meant to sound as similar as possible and respond alike to controllers, so a MIDI file made on one GM device plays much the same on another — "paint by numbers" music where the file carries both events and sounds. For synthesis GM offers little; sounds are often unedited sample replay. **Yamaha's XG** and **Roland's GS** add extra sounds/controls (similar but not fully cross-compatible).
- **GM versions** — **GM2** (1999) is an enhanced superset (the original was renamed **GM1**), backward-compatible with GM1; **GM Lite** (2001) is a reduced spec for devices such as mobile phones. All keep the core concept: a standard set of synthesis capabilities for consistent file playback.
- **Three kinds of music file** — **representational** (MIDI/SMF files describe which notes, which sounds, and how they're played — editable, like abstracted notation; changing the bass sound is just a mapping change, even to a whistle); **waveform** (MP3, WAV, AIFF store the actual audio — performance information is implicit and very hard to edit); and **mixed** (MOD and MIDI DLS combine notation with samples — small files, good reproduction, and good where synthesis is limited, as Yamaha's **Vocaloid** vocal software showed for singing). The future of music files is seen as the mixed type.

## 8. On-Board Effects

- **From outboard to integral** — in the 1970s built-in effects were the exception (reverb/chorus/echo were added by outboard units in the studio). Built-in processors first appeared in 1980s polyphonic **hybrid** synthesizers as a cheap way to disguise single DCOs instead of dual VCOs — chorus so improved string and piano sounds that it ended the separate "string machine." By the start of the twenty-first century effects had become an integral part of the synthesizer rather than an afterthought.
- **Effect types (roughly chronological)** — **reverb** (pre-delay, then early reflections, then decaying reverberation; boundaries set the timbre; nearly ubiquitous and approximable by an envelope whose release lengthens as level falls); **chorus** (cyclic detuning via a few tens of ms of modulated delay, mixed with the dry sound, like a violin section); **echo** (a delayed, attenuated repeat, hundreds of ms, with feedback for repeats); **ADT** (automatic double tracking — very short echoes that thicken but add a metallic comb-notch coloration; chorus with the LFO off); **phasers and flangers** (delayed/undelayed mix with feedback forming moving **comb-filter** notches — phasers' notches are harmonically related, flangers' are constant-frequency-spaced); **ring modulation** (sum-and-difference frequencies, metallic/robotic, good on drums); **exciters, compressors, auto-wah** (frequency-dependent distortion, dynamic-range reduction, envelope-following filters); **pitch-shifters** (fixed shift by storing at one rate and reading at another — subtle chorus or transposition); and **distortion** (adds harmonics to monophonic sounds but produces discordant inharmonic intermodulation on polyphonic sounds).
- **Multi-timbral effects** — a synthesizer multiplexes one voice circuit across notes, but an effects processor needs a complete dedicated section per timbre it processes, since stand-alone units handle only one stereo signal, not up to 16. The progression went from a single **global FX bus** shared across parts, to a "global plus individual" mix (mid-1990s global reverb/chorus plus per-part processors), to true **multi-timbral effects** (e.g. Korg's 1995 Trinity) where each part keeps the same effect alone or within a stack, making the effect part of the synthesizer's modifier section. Early integral effects were mono-in/stereo-out; true stereo-in/stereo-out integral effects appeared only in the 1990s.
- **Software effects** — late-1990s ordinary microprocessors matched the DSP chips of years before, but moving audio effects from hardware to software needed audio I/O, audio (not just MIDI) sequencers, enough processing power, and fast disks plus cheap RAM. The final enabler was the **plug-in** — software requiring a **host** to supply inputs, outputs, and services, essentially a software piece of outboard gear. A host supports a plug-in in six parts: initialization, audio input, audio output, control signals, user interface, and termination. An early ancestor was Digidesign's Turbosynth (1988); Steinberg's VST (1996) was the first audio plug-in standard, followed by incompatible rival formats.
- **Using effects** — make effective use through careful, frugal choice (a deep flange on everything impresses only briefly): reverb gives space and distance; chorus adds movement to static pads; echo enhances rhythm and syncopation; flanging is distinctive and used sparingly; pitch-shifting works as subtle chorus or, with feedback, glissando/portamento; distortion adds electric-guitar realism (now a cliché). The output user-interface metaphor is almost always the **mixer**, with per-part level/pan/EQ and (unless effects are multi-timbral) effects **send** and **return** controls summed across parts.

## 9. Editing Sounds

- **Two editing contexts** — **live parameter control** (quick, constrained edits during performance, usually a performance controller — mod wheel, foot pedal — assigned to one parameter like filter cut-off or release time so nothing else changes inadvertently) and **programming sounds** (detailed preparation, via front panel or MIDI from editor software).
- **Front panel vs. computer editing** — the front panel is fast and needs no extra gear but has a small display poor for many interrelated parameters; computer editing has a large screen and richer interface but needs a computer, cabling, and is limited by screen-update and transfer speed. The smaller the screen, the greater the computer's advantage for beginners; advanced users wanting quick edits may prefer the front panel.
- **The author's two editing principles** — *never use a preset sound* (some clear all user memories to force new sounds, though detailed sample reproductions like grand piano can be used unedited) and *never use the same note twice* (vary tone/intonation slightly to emulate the imperfect, "human" unpredictability of real instruments).
- **Mental models** — good editing requires a mental model of how the synthesis technique makes sound (without it, you change parameters at random, as randomizing editors prove by producing mostly unusable sounds) *and* a model mapping that technique to the user interface's hierarchical pages, which often don't follow the sound flow; holding two mismatched models at once is tiring and discourages editing.
- **Quick S&S edits** — because S&S instruments are full of presets and samples, they tempt replay-only use, so quick edits make them true synthesizers: **sample changes** (a different sample radically changes the source; combining contrasting "familiar" and "alien" timbres can give a new composite or a "halo"; LA-style attack-from-one/sustain-from-another, as in Roland's D50); **pitch changes** (explore extreme transpositions, fix the pitch of looped rhythmic samples, use pitch envelopes and slow LFO detune on one part); **envelopes** ("use the wrong envelopes" — swap or contrast them to turn sustained into percussive sounds or build evolving cross-fades); and **filters** (cut-off is the most powerful timbre control; remove or enhance the sample "fingerprint"; resonance acts as a selectivity control approaching self-oscillation — source of the 1970s "synth brass" filter-sweep cliché).
- **Using factory sounds** — **factory presets** demonstrate an instrument to a buyer but rarely suit a real mix; editing them teaches the synthesizer. Typical fixes: reduce excessive reverb/chorus/detune, narrow over-wide stereo, calm auto-pan, shorten over-long pad attack/release, trim release times that steal polyphony, lower over-resonant filters and over-fast filter modulation, avoid recognizable echoed sample loops, tone down over-emphasized "hyper-reality" ephemera (string buzzes, harpsichord jack-clicks), and replace stacked parallel chords with simpler, purer sounds.

## 10. Managing and Storing Sounds

- **Background management** — producing and arranging sounds are foreground work, but sounds and music are a synthesist's main resources and need cataloging, storage, and retrieval; analogue gear without digital storage may need paper notes, but the principles are the same.
- **Single point of failure** — putting all sound-creating capability on one computer makes it a single point of failure, where previously the risk was spread across many synths, samplers, drum machines, effects, and paper notes.
- **Sorting and naming** — split the pool into categories (generalized terms like *bright/dark*, instrument references like *piano-like/bell-like*, or mood references); but categories are unwieldy daily, so simple memorable names matter. Most instruments use a **bank-and-number** system whose codes (e.g. *4-12*, *B5*) don't suggest sounds, so a useful technique splits a name into a 2-3 character group prefix plus an abbreviated description (e.g. *EP:ChrBelAtt*).
- **Finding, librarians, and editors** — finding reverses naming (pick a sound type, choose category words, shortlist, audition in context). **Librarian** software handles naming, categorizing, sorting, and finding, often paired with **editors**; both come as generic/universal (programmable for almost any gear) or specific (one synthesizer, cheaper but limited) versions, exchanging data via system-exclusive MIDI.
- **Storage discipline** — modern hardware uses flash/card storage; older gear uses volatile RAM that must be saved before power-down. Logical folder organization (by year/month or by project) aids retrieval; software dongles/keys and sample CDs/DVDs need safe, ideally duplicated, storage. Important sound data should live in several locations on different media — the author has had several sudden, total, warning-free hard-disk failures.
- **Sequencing, recording, and performing notes** — sequencers vary in MIDI editing depth, functional note selection, and automation storage; stacks can be made by copying a track and changing its sound (or doubling note messages to two channels, with different pans or slight timing offsets); hocketing may need careful manual selection or a second, more capable sequencer with MIDI file export/import; delayed envelopes are made by moving note events in time. When performing live, "less is more" — simple, reliable setups, factory presets plus effects, and knowing your sound names, recovery steps, and running order.
