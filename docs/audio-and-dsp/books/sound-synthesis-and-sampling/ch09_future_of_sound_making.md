## 1. Closing the Circle

- **Synthesis is nearly "complete"** — sound quality has reached close to the physical limits of the human ear; what remains is refining the details of techniques and, above all, the **parameters used to control** synthesis (the same problem raised for resynthesis).
- **Scope for timbral innovation** — there is still room for new ways to evolve timbre dynamically and avoid the predictability of low-pass filtering, the metallic textures of FM, and the purity of additive synthesis; finding viable alternatives to the **source-modifier model** is one possible area, though producing intuitive control parameters for techniques like FM or granular synthesis is hard.
- **Control is the real bottleneck** — the major outstanding work is the interfacing between performer and instrument; only when this is refined enough will electronic instruments truly join their well-developed conventional predecessors.
- **Why control is so complex** — even when the basic vibration has a well-understood mathematical model, real instruments add layers: constraints and supports with their own resonances, many playing techniques, muting/damping effects, and the performer's varied interactions — each layer adds to the model's complexity and to the difficulty of providing an appropriate controller.
- **Other synthesis fields face the same limit** — **3D scene rendering** is close to photorealism but its hard problem is controlling rendering/animation (and very realistic synthetic humans make viewers more, not less, critical — unlike the ear, which readily accepts sampled or even poorly synthesized sound); **speech synthesis** reproduces a sentence well from analyzed control values but sounds synthetic from plain text; even word processors still need much interfacing work.
- **The Theremin as both promise and warning** — a simple monophonic instrument with one pitch control and one volume control that looks easy to play but is in fact extremely hard, illustrating how difficult intuitive control really is.
- **Control as the long-term problem** — the author's analogy: he can rent a high-performance sports car but cannot drive it skillfully enough to impress anyone — the limit is control and interfacing, not generation technology.

## 2. Control

- **Not about acronyms or bigger sample sets** — the future does not lie with marketing acronyms, ever-larger ROM sample sets, or networked CD/DVD/Blu-Ray disks of pre-packaged sounds.
- **Flexible, software-based, multi-method synthesis** — at the end of the twentieth century the future seemed to lie with controllable flexible synthesis and mathematical **modeling**; the likely outcome is an instrument combining multiple synthesis and control methods in software rather than simulating one instrument.
- **The synthesizer as part of a larger system** — increasingly the synthesizer is a component within a bigger music-making (and ultimately "multimedia") system that integrates control, sequencing, recording, composition, performance, and arrangement into a whole where the performer is an active, controlling part.
- **Multi-core processing** — general-purpose chips (with DSP-like enhancements such as Intel's MMX) and especially **multi-core** chips raise processing power without ever-rising clock speed; a future reconfigurable system of cores could be limited only by core count and memory, converging on a flexible "multimedia" computer that matches sound-making needs by convergence rather than design.
- **Synthesis and recording converge** — the same processors that produce sounds also process them, so PC-based systems increasingly offer a fully digital path from creation through sequencing, mixing, and effects to multi-channel output; **Digital Audio Workstations (DAWs)** spread downmarket as storage prices fall, and the term "hard disk" recording may fade as flash memory displaces mechanical disks.
- **MIDI's diminishing centrality** — MIDI reached an adequate stable point and was vital while low-bandwidth serial links forced the abstraction between notes and sounds, but is now less important where synthesizers, mixers, effects, and sequencing need not be separate; it survives mostly hidden inside USB, connecting performance controllers and carrying internal plug-in messaging.
- **The "orchestra in a box" and beyond** — before mid-century the synthesizer will become more like the public's "orchestra in a box," and more still: part of a multimedia facility handling images as easily as audio, with software "expert" systems assisting composition and arrangement, so the user becomes a combination of performer and director while creative human input remains essential at the start and end.

## 3. Commercial Imperatives

- **Technology follows cost** — synthesizer evolution has been driven by what was cheap, available, and well understood at each time: 1950s analogue circuitry (digital was new and costly), with **subtractive synthesis** chosen as a cost-effective way to make many sounds simply, and the organ keyboard adopted as cheap, available organ technology.
- **Monophony then digital cost breakthroughs** — early instruments were kept monophonic to cut cost (suiting 1970s progressive-rock melody lines); **FM** then gave the first cost-effective digital sound generation (a small sine-wave ROM plus numerical processing, reused across notes), offering greater polyphony and pushing polyphonic analogue synths into collector items.
- **Falling memory prices reshape the market** — **S&S** (samples and synthesis) maximized cheap ROM/RAM with short-looped samples plus filtering and enveloping; as memory got cheaper, sample replay gave large sound ranges so cheaply that the savings synthesis once provided were no longer needed — economics favors "boxes with keyboards and sounds from memory chips."
- **From synthesizer to workstation to commodity** — adding sequencing, mixing, and effects turned synthesizers into **workstations**, but ever-more sounds give little product differentiation since every workstation can make the same sounds; software synthesis (driven by fashion and easy real-time change) solved this and opened synthesis to ordinary computer owners.
- **Is the stage instrument still a synthesizer?** — modern flexible-architecture keyboards may be "almost not a synthesizer at all" but sophisticated sample-replay devices, since most users only make minor edits (e.g., real-time filter cutoff); commercial pressure will keep extending this replay-and-synthesis capability for live use.
- **The future is software synthesis inside software studios** — true future synthesis lies not in keyboard workstations but in **software synthesis engines** that are components within software "studios," because their complexity is limited by the user's budget rather than the designer's, allowing esoteric techniques impossible in a mass-market keyboard.
- **The future is rarely a simple extrapolation** — it is often something unexpected, derided, or discarded by the mainstream: drawing waveforms on acetate seemed to offer total freedom until the Fairlight CMI revealed its limits, and physical modeling seemed poised to replace sample replay until analogue modeling and a retro fashion won the short term.
- **The laptop and new interfaces** — the laptop driven by a non-keyboard controller could become the preferred sound-making device once an interface makes it "the next synonym for music-making"; candidate interfaces include **multi-touch** and wind/string hybrids, with the author noting the DJ's twin turntables now stand for music *creation*, not just replay.
