## 1. What Hybrid Synthesis Is

- **Hybrid synthesis** — synthesis methods that are not completely analogue nor completely digital; they mattered most during the early-1980s changeover from analogue to digital sound generation, but the underlying techniques carried over into all-digital methods. The author reserves "hybrid" for this analogue/digital mix and uses **composite synthesis** (covered in Ch 6) for layered/stacked techniques that some others call "hybrid".
- **Classification by digital/analogue roles** — hybrids can be divided by what the digital part does: (a) digital control of the parameters of an otherwise analogue synth; (b) a **DCO** (digitally controlled oscillator) with the rest of the instrument analogue; or (c) a digital oscillator with analogue modifiers plus digital control of those analogue parameters (the form used by mid-1990s "retro" analogue synths).
- **The predominant hybrid recipe** — digital sound generation and digital parameter control, feeding analogue filtering and enveloping; this puts the most appropriate technology to each task.
- **Richer raw source** — the major differentiator from pure analogue is the source sound: digital storage offers waveshapes beyond the traditional analogue set of sine, triangle, sawtooth and rectangular waves, especially in wavetable instruments. The 1970s Wasp pioneered mixing analogue with digital.
- **The hybrid philosophy lives on** — using a complicated oscillator with conventional subtractive modifiers is the basis of all **S&S** (samples and synthesis) instruments, and the modern trend of replacing analogue filters/oscillators with digital circuitry and software follows on from hybrid synthesis. Many modern "analogue" instruments are actually hybrids, if only for programmability.

## 2. Wavecycle Oscillators

- **Wavecycle** — another term for waveform, emphasizing the *cycle*; used to distinguish "static, sample-based" replay-oriented wavecycle oscillators from "dynamic, loop-based" wavetable oscillators. The continuum runs from a single repeated cycle, through fixed repeating multi-cycle sequences, to a long non-repeating sequence (a sample) — differing only in length and amount of repetition.
- **Single-cycle** — produces a fixed waveform repeated identically (like an analogue VCO) but usually with a far larger choice of shapes; each cycle equals the last, so a fixed timbre is the norm and downstream modifiers add interest.
- **Slider scanning** — an early user-programmable waveform method: the oscillator runs at 8 or 16 times the note frequency, driving a counter that controls a multiplexer which "scans" across 8 or 16 slider controls, summing their voltages into one cycle. A **Johnson counter** switches each slider in turn; a back-and-forth scan merely appends a time-reversed copy.
- **Memory + DAC** — instead of physical sliders, waveform values are stored in ROM (preset) or RAM (user-definable) and clocked through a **DAC** (digital-to-analogue converter) by a counter; low-order counter bits cycle through one waveform while high-order bits select which stored cycle is used. Very memory-economical, allowing many waveforms.
- **Counter-derived and pseudo-random waveforms** — feeding a counter's output straight to the DAC produces shapes set by the counter type (a binary counter gives a staircase sawtooth; up–down, Johnson and Gray-code counters give others). **Pseudo-random sequence generators** use digital feedback to create long, unpredictable number sequences for noise-like waveforms; shortening the sequence gives a definite pitch tied to the sequence length.
- **Walsh functions** — waveshapes built by adding together square/pulse waves at different rates (the digital analogue of additive synthesis with sine waves); easiest to realize by pre-calculating and storing the values in memory.
- **Output filtering** — digitally generated shapes have flat segments and abrupt transitions that add unwanted high harmonics, so a low-pass filter smooths the output; because the oscillator's pitch changes, the **VCF** must track it, usually by sharing the same control voltage as the VCO. Switching the filter in and out exposes whether an apparent sine actually carries extra harmonics.
- **Velocity switching** — mapping different waveshapes to note velocity (hard = bright, soft = dull); unlike a velocity-controlled filter it can alter just a few harmonics or change timbre several times across the velocity range, moving modification into the source and yielding dynamic sounds from modest hardware.
- **Multi-cycle** — outputs several different cycles in sequence, but the *same set repeats continuously* (unlike a sample, which may play through only once). Concatenating cycles adds extra low-frequency harmonics at sub-multiples of the fundamental; an unequal silence-to-cycle ratio can pitch-shift the perceived fundamental by non-octave intervals, giving bell-like or clangorous timbres. Symmetry tricks (reversing or inverting alternate cycles) expand the palette from a small memory.
- **Samples** — a sequence so long it does not repeat during a note. Memory is the constraint: 16-bit mono audio at 44,100 samples/s needs \~86 kB per second (\~1 MB per 12 seconds), so 1970s–80s hybrids used very short samples; cheap memory in the 1990s made sampling widespread, by then all-digital.

## 3. Wavetable Synthesis

- **Wavetable synthesis** — like multi-cycle wavecycle synthesis, it sequences stored cycles, but the crucial difference is that the actual waveform can be chosen *on a cycle-by-cycle basis*, making it more like granular synthesis than wavecycle synthesis or sample replay. The author notes wavetable is arguably the general case of which sampling is a special case, yet it saw only limited commercial success.
- **Memory-centric** — wavetable depends on memory even more than wavecycle synthesis, because the cycle in use is dynamically selected by controlling which part of memory the counter reads; the control signals effectively modulate the output shape by pointing to different table entries. The name comes from viewing memory as a "table" of cycles.
- **Swept access** — incrementing the pointer through successive cycles "sweeps" the waveshape through a series of forms; tables are arranged so similar timbres sit adjacent for smooth changes, with abrupt harmonic jumps available by sweeping across group boundaries. A common use is emulating a waveform passed through an enveloped resonant filter.
- **Random-access** — the pointer can jump to *any* cycle for each successive cycle, so any cycle can follow any other; a sweep is just a special, structured case of random access. A fixed pointer sequence behaves like a multi-cycle oscillator; a dynamically changing sequence gives the characteristic wavetable modulation.
- **Table storage types** — single-cycle, multi-cycle, samples (longer multi-cycles that may play once or partially, sometimes with multiple start points for attack-then-sustain replay), **sequence lists / wave sequences** (the ordered pointers, which can repeat, reverse, or loop), and mixed modes that combine all three in one sequence list. Naming differs by manufacturer (e.g., wavesamples, hyperwaves).
- **Multi-samples and loop sequences** — a **multi-sample** plays back samples in a different order than recorded; a **loop (wave) sequence** maps envelope segments to the looped samples within each segment (e.g., wavecycle 1 loops during attack, 2 during decay, 3 during sustain, 4 during release). This ties oscillator and envelope together, unlike analogue synthesis where the VCO and EG are independent. In samplers, "multi-samples" instead means several samples of the same sound taken at different pitches.
- **Interpolation** — gradually morphing from one wavecycle/sample to another (a cross-fade); mathematically intensive but memory-light. Interpolating raw waveform values is often musically unsatisfying because small shape changes cause large harmonic changes; interpolating between *spectra* (covered in Ch 5) gives smoother results.
- **Sample sets** — the supplied waves largely determine what an instrument can make. **General MIDI (GM)**, with its **GS** and **XG** extensions, standardizes sets around 128 sounds (forcing even pro instruments to carry bird tweets, telephone rings, etc.), and history forces in clichéd organs, harpsichords and accordions; a good (memory-hungry) piano is usually obligatory. Three sample types recur: **pitched samples** (a definite frequency), **residues** (non-pitched parts like hammer thud, fret-buzz, string-scrape), and **inharmonics** (noisy, buzzy or clangorous sounds).

## 4. DCOs (Digitally Controlled Oscillators)

- **DCO** — the digital equivalent of the analogue VCO; literally a hybrid of analogue and digital circuitry, originally developed to replace VCOs with something more pitch-stable. It can be seen as a special case of the most basic wavecycle oscillator producing only the classic sine, square, pulse and sawtooth waves. The DCO marked the last of the three-letter acronyms (VCO, VCF, LFO, VCA) before digital synths moved to plainer terms like oscillator, filter and amplifier.
- **Digitally tuned VCOs** — the simplest DCOs: a microprocessor isolates the VCO from the keyboard, measures its frequency near the upper and lower limits, then computes an **offset** voltage (to bring it into tune) and a **tracking** voltage (to set its tracking), stored in battery-backed memory. Known as **auto-tune**; early versions could take minutes to retune a full polyphonic synth, and voices that wouldn't tune could be dropped from the pool, explaining the poor reliability reputation of some early polysynths.
- **Master oscillator plus dividers** — a quartz crystal master oscillator's high-frequency clock is divided down to produce note frequencies. The **top octave** method uses 12 dividers for the highest octave's 12 notes, then successive divide-by-2 stages for lower octaves (assuming fixed equal temperament); integer division values introduce only tiny pitch errors (the ear detects \~5 cents minimum).
- **Tell-tale signs of master-oscillator DCOs** — only **global** pitch control (changing the master oscillator shifts every note), so pitch envelopes and per-voice bend/vibrato are usually absent; no per-DCO detune (achieved only by duplicating the rate adapter and dividers); often sub-oscillators (gated notes divided by 2 or 4) and chorus stand in for detuned VCOs. Many simultaneous divider outputs can induce "beehive" noise.
- **Rate adapter and jitter** — pitch-bend/vibrato is hard to get from a crystal directly, so a **rate adapter** counter occasionally removes one clock pulse to lower the effective rate; the resulting timing irregularity (**jitter**, also caused by noisy clock sources) is averaged out by the divider stages acting as filters.
- **Waveshaping** — a DCO's basic output is a pulse/square wave; to emulate classic analogue waveforms, the DCO runs at a much higher frequency and a look-up table plus DAC builds the shape. Symmetry economizes storage (a quarter cycle defines sine and triangle; a half cycle the sawtooth and pulse). 16-bit values give disproportionately better quality than 8-bit for only double the memory.
- **High-resolution DCOs** — by the 1990s, faster oscillators, programmable dividers, finer resolution and per-voice dividers removed the earlier problems; with 20 bits of divider resolution and a 62.5 kHz clock, frequency steps reach \~0.3% at 20 Hz and \~0.005% at 1 kHz. The key quality metric is the **minimum frequency step**, audible as smooth (or stepped) pitch-bend, and measurable by detuning two DCOs and timing their beats.

## 5. DCFs and the Modifier Section

- **DCF (digitally controlled filter)** — an analogue filter (often a VCF) whose cut-off frequency and Q/resonance are set digitally: a DAC converts the stored number into a control voltage. "Digital" became a marketing buzz-word after the Yamaha DX7, so DCOs and DCFs replaced VCOs and VCFs on spec sheets while "analogue" acquired connotations of "previous generation".
- **DCF vs DCO design criteria differ** — VCFs are commonly *swept*, whereas DCF notes need to stay steady while played; insufficient DAC resolution or filtering produces audible **"zipper" noise** on cut-off changes. (Earlier hybrid filters even used digitally controlled switches toggled at ultrasonic rates, with the duty cycle setting an effective variable resistance.)
- **The modifier bottleneck** — most hybrids improve the oscillator but leave the modifier as a simple resonant low-pass VCF plus VCA and envelope generator (EG), so the source carries the timbre and the famous **resonant filter sweep** cliché persists. Filtering only improved significantly in some all-digital S&S synths.

## 6. S&S (Samples and Synthesis)

- **S&S** — a generic term for methods that use a sample-playback oscillator as the raw source feeding a VCF/VCA modifier section; the samples are normally stored in ROM using **PCM** (pulse code modulation). The source behaves like a DCO driving a large wavetable; the modifiers are the subtractive structure of analogue synths. S&S is not necessarily digital — the tape-based Mellotron, Chamberlin and Birotron can be considered S&S using magnetic tape instead of solid-state memory.
- **From hybrid to all-digital** — cheap high-capacity ROM drove the shift from simple wavecycle DCOs to sample-replay with hundreds of sounds, and digital modifiers gradually replaced analogue ones; S&S thus starts as a hybrid (DCO sample replay + analogue filters) and ends fully digital, by the early 2000s pairing sampler features with detailed emulations of 1970s resonant VCFs.
- **The fingerprint problem** — S&S samples are normally replayed singly, not sequenced, so the source is a fixed set of preset sounds in ROM. Each sound's distinctive features act like an audio "fingerprint": a violin still sounds like a violin despite envelope and filter changes, and a low-pass-only filter adds its own recognizable signature. Escaping the fingerprint needs more than simple replay — sequencing, cross-fading and wave-mixing (as on the Korg Wavestation) or pitch-shifting residues into new attacks.
- **Counters and memory** — replay uses a digital counter to step through sample values held serially in memory. Samples are addressed by **start/stop** addresses (start preloads the counter, stop halts it) or **start/length**, with an **offset** parameter to begin mid-sample; E-mu's Proteus implements start, offset and length plus a partially ordered ROM for wavetable-like use. Start/stop points chosen away from zero-crossings cause clicks, so some instruments restrict them to near-zero values.
- **Loops** — looping the section between addresses provides continuous sound, most often a **sustain loop** during the EG's sustain, but also attack or release loops to extend notes without long samples. Loops can be forward, alternating forward/backward, or inverted on alternate passes to minimize clicks at non-zero loop points.
- **Variable vs fixed frequency playback** — **variable frequency** clocks sample output at a rate set by an oscillator/DCO; it is conceptually simple but the half-sampling frequency tracks pitch (so a steep tracking reconstruction VCF, 90 dB/octave or more, is needed) and it is monophonic per circuit. **Fixed frequency** keeps one sample rate and calculates the missing values, so it can be polyphonic, feed a digital modifier, and convert to analogue only after all processing — now almost exclusively used in digital synths and samplers.
- **Interpolation and pitch shifting** — fixed-frequency playback computes intermediate sample values. **Linear interpolation** joins points with straight lines (cheap, lower quality); **polynomial / differential interpolation** fits a curve (an n-point fit needs an (n−1)th-degree polynomial), giving better quality at higher cost. **Decimation** crudely drops or repeats samples for octave shifts; **oversampling** (2× up to 64× or more) supplies extra closely-spaced points to improve interpolation.
- **Quality factors** — sample reproduction quality depends on the sample rate (bandwidth), sample size in bits (signal-to-noise ratio), the interpolation technique (distortion when transposing), and the anti-aliasing/reconstruction filters. 16-bit at 44.1 kHz (CD) or 48 kHz (DAT) became the norm, with the trend toward more digital processing and less analogue electronics.

## 7. Topology

- **Topology** — how a hybrid's parts are connected; because their samples are pre-packaged, wavecycle/wavetable/S&S instruments are sometimes dismissed as mere sample-replay devices rather than true synthesizers, yet they typically offer more routing flexibility than a fixed-path analogue synth.
- **Common S&S configurations** — beyond the basic single source → single modifier, most S&S synths offer either two sound sources sharing one modifier, or two complete source-and-modifier sets; some trigger several samples from one note event, allowing samples to be combined.
- **Polyphony trade-off** — polyphony is almost always traded against topology complexity: doubling the source-and-modifier resources roughly halves the polyphony, so polyphony rose over time (a typical early-2000s S&S synth quotes 128 notes, dropping to 32 or 16 with demanding sounds).
- **Attack-and-sustain model** — early S&S used one sample for the attack and a simplified subtractive-style section for the sustain, cross-faded between them; later designs used two sample-replay sources for a more flexible division of roles.

## 8. Implementations Over Time

- **1970s** — hybrids just emerging: VCOs enhanced with auto-tune and digital control plus whole-synth programmability; two categories existed — versatile mono/poly synths, and top-octave-chip "electronic pianos"/"string machines" (the latter already declining). Limited 8-bit wavecycle waveforms, 20–30 kHz sample rates, LED displays, 4/8-bit microcontrollers, and analogue CV/Gate (or proprietary) interfacing.
- **1980s** — Yamaha's all-digital FM synths pushed rivals to ship hybrids while building their own digital instruments; DCOs replaced VCOs (portamento was an early casualty, later restored), wavetable joined wavecycle (8/12-bit), the display (a 16×2 LCD) displaced front-panel knobs via "parameter access", and interfacing polarized rapidly to MIDI after its 1983 launch.
- **1990s** — all-digital instruments and a "retro" analogue resurgence, though many "analogue" instruments were really hybrids with DCOs; ASIC-based DCOs with accumulator/divider fine resolution, 16-bit waveforms at 44.1 kHz, larger LCDs and emerging GUIs. By the mid-1990s, software emulations replaced even the VCF, beginning the "emulation" era and pricing analogue designs out by the decade's end.
- **2000s** — the hybrid was squeezed out between analogue *modeling* and *retro* analogue; modeled-analogue synths use DCO-based sample-replay to play back samples that are mathematical models of 1970s VCOs, and surviving wavetable techniques folded into all-digital S&S instruments.

## 9. Environment: Mixers, Sequencing, Recording, Performing

- **Hybrid mixers (automation)** — mixers gained digital functions too: simple MIDI-controlled line-level submixers (e.g., the Simmons SPM8:2, noted for zipper noise and an awkward interface), and motorized faders for **automation**. The core problem of adding automation to an analogue mixer is moving a physical control to match a stored value; full store-and-recall became practical only once mixers went all-digital or replaced their controls with digital ones.
- **Sequencing** — digital control means hybrids usually carry MIDI in/out, needing a MIDI sequencer (hardware or software) or a CV/Gate sequencer plus converter. Pre-MIDI proprietary buses persist on some instruments (Roland's DCB on the Juno-106, Oberheim's Parallel Bus Interface), and MIDI's sync differs from DIN-Sync 24, so "hybrid era" wiring is a more complex mix of cables and converter boxes than the pure-analogue CV/Gate era.
- **Recording** — better temperature stability means less time tuning, and the polyphony lets polyphonic parts be recorded. A hidden benefit of recording via MIDI: slowing playback to hear or play a difficult part does not change pitch (unlike analogue tape, where speed and pitch are linked), which helped drive MIDI's adoption and the home-studio-then-pro-studio workflow of the late 1980s.
- **Performing** — being polyphonic, hybrids tend to sit lower in a keyboard stack, replacing string synths, electric pianos or organs, and their broad timbral range and onboard subtractive modifiers also let them serve as solo lead instruments; built-in memories make rapid sound changes between songs easy.

## 10. Example Instruments

- **Fairlight CMI Series I (1979)** — an Australian instrument combining computer technology with sampling via hybrid analogue/digital voice cards; early plug-in 8-bit wavecycle/wavetable cards became 16-bit sample-replay by the Series III (1985). Offered additive synthesis, draw-your-own-waveform (via light-pen), step-time rhythm programming and more, at a high price.
- **PPG Wave 2.2 and Waveterm (1982)** — a German hybrid combining wavetable oscillators (1800 basic waveforms) with analogue filtering and enveloping; the Waveterm added sampling (only 8-bit) and sequencing. Later Wave 2.3 and EVU models were 12-bit. PPG and Waldorf were the main commercial exploiters of hardware wavetable synthesis.
- **Roland Juno-60 (1982)** — low-cost polyphonic synthesis using DCOs (one DCO per voice, no velocity sensing, arpeggios instead of portamento, Roland's proprietary DCB instead of MIDI); the Juno-6 was its memory-less version.
- **Roland D50 (1987)** — arguably the first commercial S&S synth, though it called the technique **LA (linear arithmetic)** and implemented it only partially: an analogue-emulation part (classic waveforms → resonant filter + digital VCA) plus a more primitive sample-replay part (digital VCA, no filtering), typically using the sample for the attack and the synth part for the sustain. One of the first polysynths with comprehensive built-in effects (EQ, chorus, reverb) and a shift from front-panel-as-guide to mental models; the last of the first-generation hybrids before the Korg M1 (the probable first *full* S&S, lacking resonant filters) ended hybrids for a while.
- **Waldorf MicroWave (1989)** — essentially a PPG Wave-type wavetable synthesizer redesigned for late-1980s electronics, with a minimalist front panel relying on a large data-entry wheel and a few buttons.
