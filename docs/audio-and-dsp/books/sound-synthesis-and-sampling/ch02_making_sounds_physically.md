## 1. Classifying Instruments and Sounds

- **Why classify** — classification systems for instruments and sounds are the entry point to analyzing, and then synthesizing, sound.
- **By performer's role** — vocal/choral (the human vocal tract) vs. instrumental/orchestral (interacting with an instrument or object); but humans also make a cappella or speech effects (clicks, pops, whistling, noise) that fall outside this division.
- **By instrument material** — strings, wind, percussion (with refinements like brass and keyboard). Widely used in the West, but it breaks down for synthesis: a keyboard synth and a wind-controlled synth would be classed differently despite being the same instrument.
- **Hornbostel–Sachs (by what vibrates)** — the ethnomusicologists' scheme, organized by the vibrating element:
  - **Idiophones** — the instrument's body vibrates (marimba, bells, wood block, triangle).
  - **Membranophones** — a tensioned membrane vibrates (drums, and the kazoo).
  - **Chordophones** — one or more strings vibrate (guitar, violin, harp, piano).
  - **Aerophones** — a column of air vibrates (oboe, flute, trombone, whistle).
  - **Electrophones** — a loudspeaker vibrates (electronic instruments); the electric guitar stays a chordophone because the vibrating string is the source.
- **Dimensionality of vibration** — aerophones and chordophones vibrate something long and thin (1D) → strong resonance and a pure, specific pitch; membranophones vibrate in 2D and idiophones in 3D → more complex, weaker resonance and a more diffuse pitch.
- **Synthesizers resist classification** — easily dismissed as electrophones, but a synth's sound may be mathematically modeled on any of the other four types, so it should arguably be classed by what it models.
- **Classifying sounds, not instruments** — needed for non-instrumental sounds (sirens, gunshots, explosions) and synthetic ones (bleeps, pips); onomatopoeia (bang, pop, hiss) helps, but pure synthetic sounds resist verbal description.

## 2. How Vibration Is Caused

- **Hit / scrape / twang / blow** — within each instrument family the vibration can be caused in different ways, and the book classifies using both the vibrating part and the method.
- **Hit** — likely the first human sound interaction and the source of percussion; hitting a string (the piano, the guitar hammer-on) is a relatively recent idea, and even air can be hit (the hand-clap, the whip crack).
- **Scrape** — needs skill and a resonating body (jazz brushes on a drum skin, bowing a string).
- **Twang** — twanging strings leads (via trying to make the sound louder) to resonators and eventually opens the way to scraping strings; modern outlets include the ruler and the African thumb piano.
- **Blow** — blowing over or through tubes (and whistling through the lips) transfers energy via the turbulence created as air hits a hard edge — not unlike scraping.

## 3. Mechanical Sequencing, Recording, and Performance

- **Sequencing** — physical instruments are "sequenced" by humans (a conductor, bell-ringing patterns) or by mechanisms (musical-box pins on a cylinder, fairground-organ linkages); mechanical timing uses a governor to hold tempo. Brass is hard to automate because the player's lips can't easily be replaced.
- **Recording** — written notation captures note start, duration, and pitch in transferable form; mechanical recording turns sound waves into the movement of a pen or gouge (needing a horn to gather sound) — the first recordings were made to analyze the singing voice, not for commerce.
- **Event vs. sound recording** — musical boxes and Jacquard-style punched tablets record the *events*; player-piano paper rolls record pitch, start/duration, and dynamics.
- **Performing** — live music needs a venue, performer(s), and an audience, with pitch and tempo cues to start; mechanical performance needs only the playback device and an energy source (a chiming clock plays even with no one listening).

## 4. Example Instruments

- **Hurdy gurdy** — a partly automated violin: a rosined wheel alternately grips and releases the strings (as a bow does), wedges set string length (as fingers do), and drone strings give it a bagpipe-like repertoire.
- **Barrel organ** — pins on a turned barrel open valves that route bellows air through tuned organ pipes; the human "organ grinder" controls only the tempo, not the performance.
- **Player piano (pianola)** — a punched paper roll controls the keys and must also encode dynamics; faithful capture of a real performance needed electrical methods, but carefully transcribed rolls sufficed and were mass-produced.
- **Phonautograph (1857)** — Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville's device traced sound onto a blackened plate or cylinder; the oldest known recording (an 1859 tuning fork, and an 1860 voice singing "Au Clair de Lune") was recovered much later by scanning these visual traces back into sound.
