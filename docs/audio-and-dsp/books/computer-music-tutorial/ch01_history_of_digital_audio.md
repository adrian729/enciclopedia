## 1. From Analog to Digital Recording

- **Analog recording** — the waveform stored on the medium is a physical *analogy* of the original sound; the magnetized particles on tape form a pattern resembling the sound waveform itself
- **Early milestones** — Léon Scott's phonautograph (1857) could record a waveform but not play it back; Edison's wax cylinders (1870s) added playback; Emile Berliner's disc gramophone (1887) was the precursor to the vinyl LP
- **Electrification of recording** — the 1906 triode vacuum tube launched electronics, but electronic recording became practical only in 1924; optical sound-on-film arrived in 1922; magnetic tape (the AEG *magnetophon*) was developed in 1930s Germany and spread worldwide after WWII
- **Fundamental limit of analog** — noise is inescapable, and it accumulates with every copy from one analog medium to another; this physical ceiling is what motivated the shift to digital

## 2. The Birth of Digital Audio

- **Sampling** — the core idea of digital audio: convert a continuous analog signal into discrete *time-sampled* values (*samples*), where each sample is just a number — a snapshot of the waveform's amplitude at one instant
- **Sampling (Nyquist) theorem** — relates sampling rate to audio bandwidth; named after Harold Nyquist (Bell Labs, 1928), though Cauchy stated a form of it in 1841
- **PCM** — *pulse-code modulation*, the first patented scheme (Reeves 1938) for transmitting messages in *amplitude-dichotomized, time-quantized* (digital) form; digital recording is still sometimes called *PCM recording*. Shannon's information theory (1948) underpinned digital transmission
- **First computer sound** — in the late 1950s Max Mathews's group at Bell Labs generated the first sample-based sounds from a digital computer; samples were written to tape, then played back through a custom 12-bit vacuum-tube digital-to-sound converter
- **DAC and ADC** — a *digital-to-analog converter* (DAC) turns numbers into analog voltages for playback; an *analog-to-digital converter* (ADC) encodes an analog waveform into numbers
- **Error detection and correction** — essential for a robust recording medium; applied by Sato, Blesser, Stockham, and Doi in the first practical recorders. NHK demonstrated the first one-channel digital recorder (built on a videotape mechanism); Denon soon improved on it
- **Commercialization** — the Sony PCM-1 (1977) encoded 13-bit audio onto videocassettes; the 16-bit Sony PCM-1600 (\~$40,000) quickly displaced it for professionals
- **Standard sampling rates** — the Audio Engineering Society set 44.1 and 48 kHz in the 1980s (the AES/EBU or AES3 standard), later extended to 88.2, 96, 176.4, and 192 kHz

## 3. Digital Audio Reaches the Public

- **Compact disc (CD)** — digital sound reached the public in 1982 via the CD, a 12 cm optical disc read by a laser, jointly developed by Philips and Sony; over 1.35 million players sold within two years. Its adoption as CD-ROM for software and data distribution cemented the format
- **Later disc formats mostly failed** — DVD-Audio and SACD (2000) offered better quality but never caught on; Blu-ray (2002) carries up to 24-bit/96 kHz across eight channels, but an audio-only Blu-ray (2013) also failed
- **Lossy vs lossless compression** — MP3 (MPEG Audio Layer III) uses *lossy compression*, discarding audio information during encoding to shrink files at the cost of fidelity; *lossless compression* (e.g., FLAC) shrinks files by \~50% while allowing exact reconstruction of the original

## 4. Digital Audio for Musicians and Studios

- **The DAW era** — high-quality DACs for personal computers appeared around 1988, alongside file formats SDII, AIFF, and WAVE; *digital audio workstations* (Pro Tools, Cubase, Digital Performer, Logic) let musicians record to hard disk and edit precisely on an on-screen timeline
- **Multitrack recording** — *multitrack* recorders provide discrete *tracks* recorded at different times, allowing each instrument to be captured separately and recordings to be built up in layers; the BBC built a 10-channel digital recorder (1976), 3M shipped the first commercial 32-track (1978), and Soundstream built the first disk-based random-access editor/mixer
- **Cost collapse** — early digital multitrack was extremely expensive (the Studer D820-48 sold for \~$270,000 in 1991); software DAWs soon replaced tape machines, making record/edit/mix possible on portable laptops
- **The art of recording** — proper equipment is not enough; skill is learned through apprenticeship. Four-year *Tonmeister* degrees train recording engineers in both music and applied physics (room acoustics, microphone types and techniques, production methods)
