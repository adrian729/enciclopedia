## 1. Terrains and Trajectories

- **Wave terrain (WT) synthesis** — extends wavetable lookup (chapter 6) from a 2-D table to scanning a three-dimensional \((x, y, z)\) surface, like mountains and valleys; the height \(z\) at a scanned \((x, y)\) position becomes the waveform's amplitude sample. Also called *two-variable function* synthesis because the surface can be a *closed-form function of two variables*
- **Origins** — first explored by Rich Gold (with Leonard Cottrell), Mitsuhashi (1982), and Borgonovo and Haus (1984, 1986); described in Csound by Comajuncosas and Mikelson (2000); the most comprehensive treatments are Stuart James's master's and doctoral theses (2005, 2015)
- **Character** — sounds are generally synthetic; early experiments focused on drones with continuously mutating timbres. James (2005) showed WT can emulate additive, vector, AM/ring modulation, phase distortion, FM, and waveshaping synthesis under certain conditions
- **Trajectory** — the path a scan takes over the terrain; unlike a traditional left-to-right repeating wavetable scan, it can be any sequence of points (straight/curved line, zig-zag, random walk, sinusoidal, elliptical) and may or may not repeat. Earlier literature called this an *orbit*, but that wrongly connotes an elliptical function
- **What produces sound** — pitched tones come from *periodic repetition* of trajectories, the repetition frequency setting the fundamental; no movement or a flat terrain gives silence. Undulation frequency sets spectral content, undulation depth sets amplitude, and jagged angles generate more high frequencies — though precise spectrum also depends on the scanning frequencies

## 2. Generating Predictable Waveforms

- **Any surface works** — terrains range from tightly constrained math functions to arbitrary topography; one project used the Earth's global Digital Elevation Model as a terrain
- **Discrete vs. continuous** — *discrete* terrains store values sampled at discrete surface points; *continuous* terrains are computed on the fly to arbitrary precision by math functions; most terrains in practice are discrete
- **Why simple functions** — as with FM and waveshaping, simple mathematical terrains let you analytically predict the exact output waveform and spectrum for a given terrain and trajectory
- **Conditions for predictability** — over the range \([-1 \le x \le 1, -1 \le y \le 1]\): (1) the \(x\) and \(y\) functions and their first-order partial derivatives must be continuous over the terrain; (2) the functions must be zero on the terrain boundaries — ensuring continuity when the trajectory wraps from one edge to another (analogous to one-index wavetable wraparound). Condition 2 can be ignored if the trajectory stays within the edges
- **Example terrain** — \( \text{wave}(x, y) = \sin(\pi/2 \times x) \times \sin(\pi/2 \times y) \) satisfies both conditions. Other investigated functions: piecewise, polynomials, fractal/iterative maps, additive and Chebyshev functions, elliptic functions, and dynamical systems; James (2005) cataloged terrain types including periodic-continuous, periodic-discontinuous, undefined-value, tending-to-infinity, and wavetable/tiled forms

## 3. Trajectory and Terrain Motion

- **Periodic trajectories** — the output depends on both terrain and trajectory; a repeating (periodic) trajectory yields a periodic output signal. An elliptical trajectory is generated by sinusoidal terms in both dimensions, e.g. \( x = 0.5 \times \sin(8\pi t + \pi/5) \), \( y = \sin(8\pi t) \)
- **Time-varying trajectories** — a fixed trajectory gives a fixed waveform with a static spectrum; changing the trajectory over time (via real-time input devices or programmed automation) generates a time-varying waveform
- **Time-varying terrain** — alternatively, fix the trajectory and vary the terrain itself, like tracing undulations on the surface of the sea; this is the idea behind *scanned synthesis* (chapter 18)

## 4. Extensions and Implementations

- **Generating terrains** — James (2005) explored video capture, noise functions, recurrence plots, and spline surfaces, plus matrix operations (colored-terrain mappings, convolution blurring/sharpening/embossing, spatial mapping, video feedback); his PhD work (2015) used the WT interface to generate control data for a spatialization scheme where spectral bands follow independent spatial paths
- **Expressive control** — Zabetian (2018) built a WT instrument with MIDI polyphonic expression (MPE) via a ROLI Seaboard, plus graphic displays for interactive trajectory/terrain control and chaotic modulation of both
- **Why visualization matters** — implementations can run without a display (e.g. Csound code), but visualization greatly helps find a "sweet spot" or a smooth/jagged terrain; Zabetian's (2018) user study confirmed graphical display aids understanding
- **Software** — the `wterrain` opcode in Csound, the `terrain~` object in Max and Pd, and a Jitter implementation using `jit.peek~` (table lookup plus interpolation); Aaron Anderson's Wave Terrain Synth (2017) supports multiple simultaneous trajectories, a large set of terrains and reading methods, image/sound-to-terrain conversion, and MIDI control with note velocity tied to trajectory size
