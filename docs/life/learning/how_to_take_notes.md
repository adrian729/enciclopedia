## 1. Deep processing

Deep processing is what happens when you interact conceptually with what you are studying.

Memory strengthens depending on the type of cognitive processing used during study. The deeper the kind of processing, better the chances of long term retention.

### 1.1. Outline method

Split and organize the content in sections/items, and then expand each item knowledge summarizing the concepts.

Re-organize the items and hierarchies as needed and best for you.

The fact of thinking about the concepts when summarizing and organizing the information will increase the chance of knowledge retention.

If possible add your own examples, that will help understanding and thinking about the concepts.

Something that can be hard is finding how to organize and explain the concepts. Something that can help is:

- Trying to "explain it to someone else", or simulating doing so, to see if it makes sense.
- Writing down smaller notes and ideas (similar to brainstorming), to get some ideas, and trying to give it form afterwards.
- Just writing down and iterating over it until happy with the results.

A very important note, when we talk about summarizing we don't mean just transcribing word by word. That will just lead to superficial processing instead of thinking and going deep into the concepts.

## 2. Active recall

There is a difference between passive and active recall.

When you re-read exactly what you wrote when studying (your notes) the brain will passively recall that situation and what you were thinking at the moment.

If what you want is to reinforce what you've learned, you need to try to get your brain to reconstruct actively that information.

For example it is the difference between just recognizing someone (you see Pedro and passively your brain knows who he is), and being asked some aspects about Pedro (then your brain needs to "reconstruct" what it knows about Pedro).

### 2.1. Cornell method

The Cornell method has a specific structure layout.

1. On the header of the page, write down the `title` and `metadata`, What are we doing notes about and why.
2. Split then the page body between a 30% section at the left, and the other 70%.
   - In the 70% section, write the `notes` as before.
   - In the 30% section, write `cues`. At exam-type `questions` and `keywords`.
3. On the page footer, add a really `short summary` of the overall.

The Cornell method has the best of both worlds:

- The notes taken are basically the `outline method` already, so we work on the `deep processing`.
- The `cues` section is what helps with `active recall`. The `keywords` and `questions` will make us think about the topic, filling up the gaps ourselves.

## 3. Spaced repetition

Until this point we got two important points:

- We stopped studying superficially (just copy or transcribe) with the `outline method`.
- We stopped doing only passive recall of the topics with the `Cornell method`.

Even with all this, you will still forget most of what you study. That is normal and fine. When you study, first you will recall most of it (depending on how deeply you study), but with time you will most certainly forget things.

Unless you frequently revisit/use that information, your brain will assume it is not important and start forgetting it to focus on other topics.

To avoid that we need to trick our brain into thinking this information is more important than it appears to it.

To do so we can use `spaced repetition`. The idea is simple: revisit the information several times, with increasing intervals of time.

Do it as you want, but one way is using the `2, 3, 5, 7` technique. After the first time visiting the information, wait two days before revisiting it. Then do it 3 days later, 5, and lastly 7.

This will obviously work better or worse depending on your notes quality. You can use also other methods to revisit information (like flashcards etc).
